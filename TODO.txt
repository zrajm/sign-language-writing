  # Dimskis system

  In 1998, Lidiya Sergeyevna Dimskis published [a textbook][1] for learning the
  basics of Belarusian sign language. In the book, she introduces a system of
  conventional signs, through which she then describes all the words of sign
  language presented in the book.

  Here are some of the symbols used in the Dimskis system. Gestures are written
  from left to right, each gesture is written in the order hand, configuration,
  orientation, localization, movement. Many symbols are composite: arrows can
  have different shapes and shading, localization symbols indicate the place of
  contact.

  The book presents not only individual gestures but also complete statements,
  but does not provide means for indicating non-manual components.

  [1]: https://vk.com/wall-71630588_15225

  # Date: 2024? (visited date 2025-09-28, minus '1y').
  #
  # This post was reconstructed by copying the first part from the Quora page,
  # then for the latter part (because that is only available directly from for

  # pieces from Google search result snippets.

  > [Profile photo for Hartmut Teuber]
  > Hartmut Teuber
  > M.A. in Linguistics, Brandeis University (Graduated 1985) · Author has 894
  > answers and 207.6K answer views · 1y
  >
  > > What is the process for transcribing sign language into written words
  > > for a deaf person?
  >
  > One can do that, but only crudely. That is using English (or other
  > languages) words that are used to "label" individual signs. This is what is
  > known as "glossing". They often do not include morphological alterations on
  > signs in the transcription. They are often written in caps. A Deaf person
  > fluent in ASL will reproduce signs from the glosses and add appropriate
  > inflectional forms not represented in the glosses based on his knowledge of
  > the "grammar" of ASL. At a more advanced level, researchers will suffix the
  > them with appropriate pronouns and/or adverbs. For example, a sign with a
  > directional movement, like GIVE, showing two persons involved, such as from
  > 'him' to 'her', can be written with GIVE-him to her, or genderless from
  > "index 1" to "index 2", in short "ix1" and "ix2″. Of course, ix1 and ix2
  > are pronominal. The predicate with GIVE can be written GIVE-ix1 to ix2. If
  > an adverb is used on the sign GIVE, like "hesitantly". Altogether, the
  > transcription of this predicate will look like GIVE-hesitantly-ix1-ix2.
  > Glossing with pronouns and adverbs is not the only way to write, but it is
  > more common. There are several different notation systems, using letters
  > and numerals or own graphic symbols, like used by W.C. Stokoe (Dictionary
  > of ASL) or Valerie Sutton (SignWriting). In other countries, they have
  > their own notation systems. Myself have created a different system, using
  > letters, numerals, and diacritics, called SignLettering.
  >
  > Upvote ·. 91.

** TODO Difference between writing and notation/transcription
   A system is considered a script or functional writing system if it is
   intended for general usage. Notations, on the other hand, are primarily
   developed for academic purposes ([Bentele 2002: 4--15][Bentele 2002],
   [Coulmas 1996], [DeFrancis 1989], [Miller 1994: 196ff][Miller 1994]).

** TODO Early descriptions of handshapes (from 800-hundreds)
   The first attempt to record handshapes on paper was made in the 7th century.
   These so-called "finger numbers" or "numerical gestures" were not yet an
   attempt to write sign languages.

   Later, in the 16th and 18th centuries, further attempts were made to teach
   deaf students spoken language using the "manual alphabet", "methodical
   signs", and "sign register".
